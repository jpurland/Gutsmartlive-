Crohn's, colitis and the microbiome

6 minute read

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are the two main forms of inflammatory bowel disease, or IBD. In both, the immune system keeps attacking the gut lining. Ulcerative colitis affects the inner lining of the colon. Crohn's can strike anywhere from mouth to anus and can go through the full thickness of the bowel wall.

Nobody has found a single cause. The leading explanation is that IBD develops when three things meet: genes that make someone susceptible, something in the environment, and the bacteria living in the gut. That last part is where the microbiome comes in.

In mice, no bacteria means no colitis

Some of the clearest evidence comes from animals. Mice bred without the gene for IL-10, a signal that calms the immune system, normally develop colitis on their own. In a 1998 study in Infection and Immunity, researchers raised these same mice completely germ-free. They had no colitis and no sign of immune activation. Mice with the same genes, raised with ordinary gut bacteria, developed inflammation throughout the colon.

This shows that in these mice, the immune system needs bacteria to attack. It doesn't prove the same is true for every person with IBD, but it set the direction for much of the research that followed.

Genes that sense bacteria

In 2001, two research teams publishing in Nature found that variants in a gene called NOD2 raise the risk of Crohn's disease. Later work showed that NOD2's job is to detect a piece of bacterial cell walls, so it acts as one of the body's bacterial sensors. Many of the genes since linked to IBD are involved in the same broad task: how the gut lining and immune system recognize and handle microbes.

Genes alone don't explain IBD. Most people with risky NOD2 variants never get Crohn's, and rates of IBD have risen far too quickly in recent decades for genetics to be the whole story. Something in modern life is changing the equation, and the microbiome is a likely part of it.

A different gut community from the start

People with IBD usually have a less diverse gut microbiome. The trouble with many studies is that patients are already on medications, which can change gut bacteria themselves. A 2014 study in Cell Host & Microbe got around this by studying 447 children with newly diagnosed Crohn's disease who had not yet started treatment, compared with 221 children without IBD.

In tissue samples from the gut, the children with Crohn's had more of several bacteria linked to inflammation, including members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, and fewer of the bacteria that ferment fiber, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia and Bifidobacterium. Children who had taken antibiotics before diagnosis showed even bigger shifts.

A missing protector

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii keeps coming up. It is one of the most common bacteria in a healthy adult gut and a major producer of butyrate, a fatty acid that feeds the cells of the colon lining. In a 2008 study in PNAS, people with Crohn's who had surgery and had lower levels of this bacterium in their removed bowel tissue were more likely to see the disease come back early. In lab dishes, the bacterium released substances that dampened inflammation, and giving it to mice with chemically induced colitis reduced their inflammation.

That's a promising picture, but it is not proof that low F. prausnitzii causes Crohn's. Inflammation itself can drive protective bacteria out, so the loss could be a result of the disease as well as a contributor.

Antibiotics and risk

If gut bacteria matter, disrupting them early might matter too. A 2012 study in Pediatrics followed more than one million children in the UK. Those who received antibiotics that act on anaerobic bacteria had a higher rate of later IBD diagnosis. The link was strongest for antibiotics given in the first year of life, and more courses meant higher risk.

This is an association, not proof of cause. Children who need many antibiotics may differ in other ways, and some early symptoms of IBD may lead to antibiotic prescriptions. Antibiotics are often necessary and life-saving; the point is that they are not neutral for the gut.

What we can say with confidence: gut bacteria are deeply involved in IBD. Animal studies show bacteria can be required for colitis to develop, key risk genes are bacterial sensors, and people with IBD have a measurably different microbiome even before treatment. What researchers can't yet say is which changes come first, or whether fixing the microbiome alone can reverse the disease.
