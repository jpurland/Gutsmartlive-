Obesity & Your Gut Part 2 of 6

Your gut bacteria and your weight: the evidence

8 minute read

Your large intestine holds tens of trillions of bacteria, roughly as many as the cells in the rest of your body. Together they carry far more genes than you do, and many of those genes handle food you can't digest on your own. Over the past 20 years a series of experiments has shown that this community has a real say in body weight.

Mice without bacteria stay lean

In 2004 researchers at Washington University compared mice raised completely free of germs with ordinary mice. The germ-free mice carried about 40 percent less body fat, even though they ate more. When the scientists gave them gut bacteria from normal mice, their body fat rose by about 60 percent within two weeks, while they ate less than before.

Obese bacteria make mice fatter

The same group then took gut bacteria from obese mice and from lean mice and transferred each into germ-free mice. On identical food, the mice that received the obese donors' bacteria gained significantly more fat. Those bacteria were better at extracting calories from the diet, leaving less energy behind in the stool. The trait traveled with the microbes.

The human twin experiment

A 2013 study in Science made the link to people. Researchers found pairs of human twins in which one twin had obesity and the other didn't, then transferred each twin's gut bacteria into germ-free mice. Mice given the obese twin's bacteria gained more fat and showed obesity-related metabolic changes. Mice given the lean twin's bacteria didn't.

The most striking part came next. When the two groups of mice were housed together, bacteria from the lean side moved in and prevented the extra fat gain. But that protection only worked when the mice ate a diet high in fiber and low in saturated fat. On a typical Western-style diet, the lean bacteria couldn't take hold. Diet and microbes worked together.

What people's guts show

A 2013 study in Nature analyzed the gut bacteria of 292 Danish adults. About a quarter had low bacterial richness, meaning fewer kinds of bacteria and fewer bacterial genes. That group had more body fat, more insulin resistance, worse cholesterol and more inflammation, and those with obesity gained more weight over the following years. Low diversity has shown up in many studies since as a marker of poor metabolic health.

Early work also suggested that people with obesity carry a different balance of the two main bacterial families, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes. Later studies were mixed on that particular ratio. Researchers now focus more on diversity and on specific bacteria and the chemicals they make.

What this does and doesn't prove

  • Proven in animals: gut bacteria can cause fat gain, and an obesity-prone microbiome can be transferred.
  • Strong in people: obesity and poor metabolic health consistently come with a less diverse, altered microbiome.
  • Not yet proven in people: that changing the microbiome alone produces major weight loss. Human transplant trials have improved some blood sugar measures but haven't caused meaningful weight loss so far. Part 6 covers those trials.

The fair summary: your gut bacteria are an active player in obesity, not a bystander. They're one important part of a disease with several causes, and one of the parts you can actually influence every day through food.
