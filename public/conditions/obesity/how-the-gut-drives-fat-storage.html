Obesity & Your Gut Part 3 of 6

How an unbalanced gut drives fat storage

8 minute read

If gut bacteria influence weight, how? Researchers have pinned down several pathways. None of them involve willpower. All of them help explain why visceral fat, the fat around the organs, is so closely tied to gut health.

1. Squeezing extra calories from food

Gut bacteria ferment fiber and other carbohydrates your own enzymes can't break down, turning them into compounds your body can absorb. Some bacterial communities are better at this than others. In a 2011 study at the National Institutes of Health, shifting how much food people ate changed their gut bacteria within days, and a rise in Firmicutes bacteria was linked to absorbing about 150 more calories a day. Spread over months, a difference that small adds up.

2. A leaky gut that keeps the body inflamed

The gut lining is supposed to keep bacteria and their debris inside the intestine. Some bacteria carry a molecule on their outer wall called lipopolysaccharide, or LPS, which sets off a strong immune reaction if it reaches the blood. In a 2007 study in Diabetes, a high-fat diet raised blood LPS levels in mice two- to threefold. Infusing small amounts of LPS directly was enough to cause weight gain, insulin resistance and fatty liver. Researchers call this "metabolic endotoxemia."

This matters for visceral fat in particular. Blood from the intestines drains first through the portal vein to the liver, passing the fat stores around the organs. Low-grade inflammation coming from the gut lands right where visceral fat sits, and inflamed visceral fat in turn releases more inflammatory signals. It becomes a loop.

3. Appetite signals that go quiet

When bacteria ferment fiber they produce short-chain fatty acids, mainly acetate, propionate and butyrate. These compounds tell specialized cells in the gut wall to release GLP-1 and PYY, hormones that make you feel full and help control blood sugar. GLP-1 is the same hormone that the new weight-loss medications mimic.

A 2015 trial in the journal Gut tested this directly in people. Overweight adults took a supplement that delivered propionate straight to the colon. They ate about 14 percent fewer calories at a test meal, gained less weight over 24 weeks than the comparison group, and kept fat around their abdominal organs from building up. A gut starved of fiber, or missing the bacteria that ferment it, sends weaker fullness signals.

4. Switches that tell the body to store fat

Gut bacteria also influence genes that control fat storage. The 2004 germ-free mouse research found that bacteria suppress a protein that normally restrains fat storage, making it easier for the body to pack calories into fat cells. Bacteria also reshape bile acids, which act as hormones that affect blood sugar, fat burning and liver fat.

Putting it together

An unbalanced, low-diversity gut can pull more calories from the same food, keep the body mildly inflamed, mute the hormones that say "you're full," and nudge the body toward storing fat. Each effect is modest. Together, running every day for years, they push weight and visceral fat up. None of that is a moral failure. It's biology, and it's also biology that responds to what you feed it.
