Why the weight comes back after a diet

6 minute read

Most people who lose weight on a diet regain much of it within a few years. That pattern is often blamed on people "giving up." The research tells a different story: after weight loss, the body and its gut bacteria actively work to restore the lost fat.

Hunger hormones stay switched on

In a 2011 study in the New England Journal of Medicine, Australian researchers put adults with obesity on a 10-week very-low-calorie diet. A full year later, their hormones were still pushing them to regain. The hunger hormone ghrelin stayed higher, several fullness hormones stayed lower, and the participants reported feeling hungrier than before they started. The body treated the weight loss as a threat to be corrected.

The metabolism slows and stays slow

Researchers at the National Institutes of Health followed contestants from the TV show The Biggest Loser for six years. Most regained a large share of the weight. Their resting metabolism, though, stayed far lower than expected for their size, on average by about 500 calories a day. Their bodies were burning less energy at rest years after the show ended.

The gut "remembers" obesity

A 2016 study in Nature found the gut microbiome plays a part too. Mice were made obese, then dieted back to normal weight. Their weight and blood sugar recovered, but their gut bacteria didn't. The obesity-type microbiome lingered for months. When those mice were fed a rich diet again, they regained weight faster than mice that had never been obese.

The researchers showed the bacteria were responsible. Transferring the "post-diet" microbiome into germ-free mice passed on the faster regain. The lingering bacteria broke down certain plant compounds, called flavonoids, that help the body burn energy. Giving the mice those flavonoids after dieting reduced the rebound. This is animal research, but it points to something important: losing weight doesn't automatically repair the gut.

What this means for you

  • Regain isn't a character flaw. It's the expected biological response, and it's the main reason obesity is treated as a chronic disease rather than a short-term problem.
  • Crash diets work against you. Severe restriction triggers the strongest hormonal push back.
  • Plan for the long run. Habits that keep feeding your gut bacteria, like fermented foods and a wide variety of plant foods, are meant to continue after the weight comes off, not stop.
  • Medical treatment is legitimate. For many people, medication or surgery is a reasonable way to counter a body that keeps defending a higher weight. Part 6 has more.
