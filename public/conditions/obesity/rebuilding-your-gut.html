Rebuilding a healthier gut: what actually helps

8 minute read

If obesity is partly a disorder of the gut microbiome, the hopeful flip side is that the microbiome changes fast. Diet shifts can reshape gut bacteria within days. Here's what the evidence supports, from everyday habits to medical treatment.

Eat something alive every day

In a 2021 Stanford trial published in Cell, adults who worked up to about six servings a day of fermented foods like yogurt, kefir, kimchi and sauerkraut for 10 weeks saw their gut bacteria grow more diverse and a wide range of inflammation markers fall. Inflammation is one of the main links between the gut and visceral fat. This is the core of the Living Diet: a fermented food with live cultures alongside every meal.

Eat a wide variety of plants

In the American Gut Project, which analyzed samples from more than 10,000 people, those who ate more than 30 different plant foods a week had more diverse gut bacteria than those who ate fewer than 10. Variety matters as much as quantity, because different bacteria thrive on different fibers. Vegetables, beans, nuts, seeds, herbs, berries and whole grains all count. Fiber also feeds the bacteria that make short-chain fatty acids, which help switch on your natural fullness hormones.

Specific strains show modest effects

Some probiotic strains have reduced visceral fat in controlled trials, most notably Lactobacillus gasseri SBT2055. The effects are modest and strain-specific. We cover these studies in what the research says about fermented foods and visceral fat.

One bacterium drawing a lot of attention is Akkermansia muciniphila, which lives in the gut's mucus layer. People with more of it tend to have healthier metabolism and respond better to calorie reduction. In a 2019 pilot trial in Nature Medicine, 32 overweight or obese adults with insulin resistance took either Akkermansia or a placebo for three months. Those taking the pasteurized form had better insulin sensitivity and lower cholesterol, and small drops in weight and fat that weren't statistically certain. It's promising but early.

Fecal transplants: not a weight-loss cure yet

If an obesity-type microbiome can cause weight gain in mice, could a lean donor's bacteria reverse it in people? In a 2012 Dutch trial, men with metabolic syndrome who received gut bacteria from lean donors had better insulin sensitivity six weeks later, but didn't lose weight. A 2020 trial at Massachusetts General Hospital gave adults with obesity capsules of lean-donor bacteria for 12 weeks. The bacteria took hold, but insulin sensitivity and weight didn't improve. Transplants aren't an approved obesity treatment, and do-it-yourself versions can spread serious infections. Don't try it outside a clinical trial.

Medical treatment isn't cheating

Because obesity is a disease, it's reasonable to treat it medically. GLP-1 medications copy a fullness hormone the gut itself makes. Bariatric surgery changes the gut so profoundly that it reshapes the microbiome. In a 2015 study in Cell Metabolism, transferring gut bacteria from women who'd had gastric bypass into germ-free mice led the mice to store less fat. Both treatments work on the same gut-to-brain system this series describes. Talk with your doctor about whether they fit your situation, and keep feeding your gut along the way.

A simple daily plan

  • Put a living, fermented food on your plate at every meal.
  • Aim for 30 different plant foods a week.
  • Cut back on ultra-processed foods, sweet drinks and artificial sweeteners.
  • Protect sleep and manage stress where you can.
  • Take antibiotics only when truly needed.
  • Track visceral fat with a waist measurement or scan, not just the scale.
  • Work with a doctor, especially if you're managing other conditions or considering medication.
Remember: you didn't cause this disease by lacking willpower, and you're not stuck with it. Feeding your gut well is one of the most direct, daily ways to push back. Read what's worked for others or share your own story.
