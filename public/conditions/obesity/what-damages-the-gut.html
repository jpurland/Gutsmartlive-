Obesity & Your Gut Part 5 of 6

What throws the gut out of balance

8 minute read

Obesity rates climbed sharply over a few decades, far too fast for human genes to have changed. What did change was the environment, especially the food supply. Much of that change lands directly on the gut microbiome.

Ultra-processed food

In a 2019 study at the National Institutes of Health, 20 adults lived in a research ward for four weeks and ate two diets, two weeks each. One was ultra-processed, the other minimally processed, and both were matched for calories, sugar, fat, fiber and salt on offer. People could eat as much as they wanted. On the ultra-processed diet they ate about 500 more calories a day and gained around two pounds in two weeks. On the minimally processed diet they lost weight. Nobody was told to restrict anything. The food itself changed how much they ate.

Too little fiber

Fiber is the main food for many beneficial gut bacteria. Most American adults eat about 15 grams a day, roughly half the recommended amount. A 2016 study in Nature found that when mice ate a low-fiber diet over several generations, kinds of gut bacteria disappeared and didn't come back when fiber returned to the diet. Lost diversity, in other words, can be hard to win back, which is one reason fermented foods that add bacteria back may help.

Common food additives

Emulsifiers keep processed foods smooth and shelf-stable. In a 2015 study in Nature, mice given two common emulsifiers, carboxymethylcellulose and polysorbate 80, developed thinner gut mucus, bacteria closer to the gut wall, low-grade inflammation, weight gain and signs of metabolic syndrome. A small follow-up trial in healthy people found carboxymethylcellulose changed gut bacteria and reduced helpful compounds within two weeks. The human evidence is early, but it's a reason to favor foods with short ingredient lists.

Artificial sweeteners

Sweeteners are sold as a way around sugar. A 2022 randomized trial in Cell gave healthy adults small daily amounts of different sweeteners for two weeks. Saccharin and sucralose changed people's gut bacteria and worsened their blood sugar response, and transferring their bacteria into mice passed the effect on. Responses varied a lot from person to person. Sweeteners aren't a clean shortcut for the gut.

Antibiotics early in life

Farmers have used low doses of antibiotics for decades to make livestock gain weight faster. A 2014 study in Cell found that giving young mice low-dose penicillin altered their gut bacteria during a key developmental window and led to more body fat later, and the effect could be transferred with the bacteria alone. Studies in children have found that antibiotic courses in infancy are linked to higher weight later in childhood. Antibiotics save lives and should be taken when truly needed. Taking them only when necessary protects the gut.

Sleep, stress and alcohol

Short sleep, chronic stress and heavy drinking all disrupt gut bacteria and weaken the gut lining in research studies. They also raise cortisol, which favors visceral fat. They're part of the same picture.

The pattern: people today live in an environment that steadily wears down the gut microbiome. That's a much better explanation for the obesity epidemic than a sudden worldwide collapse in willpower.
