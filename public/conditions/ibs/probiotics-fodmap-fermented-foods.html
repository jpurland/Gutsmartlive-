Probiotics, low-FODMAP and fermented foods for IBS: what the evidence says

6 minute read

If gut bacteria are part of IBS, it makes sense to ask whether changing them with food or supplements helps. The answer is: sometimes, for some people, and less dramatically than marketing suggests. Here is what the research supports.

First, rule out other problems. IBS is diagnosed after more serious conditions are considered. See a doctor promptly if you have blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, anemia, symptoms that wake you at night, a family history of colon cancer, celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease, or new bowel symptoms starting at age 45 or older. These are not typical of IBS.

Probiotics: mixed results

Probiotics are the most studied option, and also the most confusing. A 2018 review in Alimentary Pharmacology & Therapeutics, led by gastroenterologist Alexander Ford, pooled 53 randomized trials involving more than 5,500 adults with IBS. Some probiotic combinations and specific strains seemed to help overall symptoms and belly pain. But the trials used many different products, and the authors concluded the data were not strong enough to say which strain or combination to recommend.

Major guidelines reflect that uncertainty. The American College of Gastroenterology's 2021 IBS guideline suggests against probiotics for overall IBS symptoms, noting the evidence quality is very low. The American Gastroenterological Association's 2020 probiotics guideline recommends using them for IBS only within a clinical trial.

That doesn't mean probiotics never help anyone. It means there's no reliable way yet to predict who will benefit or which product to use. If you try one, pick a single product, give it about four weeks, and stop if nothing changes.

The low-FODMAP diet: the best-studied food approach

FODMAPs are a group of carbohydrates that the small intestine absorbs poorly. They include fructans in wheat, onion and garlic, lactose in milk, excess fructose in some fruits, and sugar alcohols. When they reach the colon, gut bacteria ferment them, producing gas, and they also draw water into the gut. In a sensitive gut, that can mean pain, bloating and diarrhea.

A key 2014 trial in Gastroenterology by Emma Halmos and colleagues at Monash University fed 30 people with IBS all of their meals for three weeks on a low-FODMAP diet and three weeks on a typical Australian diet, in random order. Overall symptom scores were about half as high on the low-FODMAP diet.

The ACG guideline recommends a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet for IBS. The word "limited" matters. The diet is meant to run in three phases: cut back on FODMAPs for a few weeks, reintroduce them one group at a time to find your personal triggers, then eat as broadly as you can tolerate.

There's a microbiome trade-off. A 2012 trial in the Journal of Nutrition found that restricting fermentable carbohydrates reduced symptoms but also lowered levels of Bifidobacterium, a group generally considered beneficial. That's one reason experts don't recommend staying on a strict version long-term. Working with a dietitian trained in the approach makes it easier and safer.

Fiber: soluble, not insoluble

The ACG guideline also suggests soluble fiber, such as psyllium, for overall IBS symptoms, and not insoluble fiber like wheat bran, which can make bloating worse for some people. Start with a small dose and increase slowly.

Fermented foods: start small

Yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi and kombucha are often recommended for gut health. For IBS specifically, there are few good trials. Some fermented foods also contain FODMAPs, such as lactose in some dairy, or ingredients like garlic and onion in kimchi, and carbonation in kombucha can add to bloating.

A reasonable approach if you want to include them: add one at a time, in a small amount, such as a spoonful of sauerkraut or a few sips of kefir, and keep a simple symptom diary. If it's well tolerated, increase slowly. If symptoms flare, back off. Fermented foods are a reasonable part of a varied diet, but they are not a proven IBS treatment.

Don't overlook the brain side

Because IBS involves the gut-brain connection, treatments aimed at that link, such as gut-directed hypnotherapy, cognitive behavioral therapy and some low-dose medications, help many people. Your doctor can talk through which options fit your symptoms. Don't stop or change any medicine on your own.

Putting it together

No diet or supplement cures IBS. The best-supported food strategy is a short, structured low-FODMAP trial followed by reintroduction, ideally with a dietitian. Soluble fiber helps some people. Probiotics and fermented foods are worth a careful trial for some, but the evidence is weak. Most people do best with a combined plan made with their doctor.
