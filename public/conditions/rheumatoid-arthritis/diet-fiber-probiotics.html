Diet, fiber and probiotics for rheumatoid arthritis: what helps

6 minute read

If gut bacteria are involved in rheumatoid arthritis, it's natural to ask whether feeding them differently could help. Researchers have tested this with fiber, probiotics and whole-diet changes. The honest summary: some approaches show real but modest benefits, none is a cure, and all of them work best as additions to medical treatment, not substitutes for it.

Why fiber is a reasonable bet

When gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, propionate and acetate. These compounds help keep the gut lining healthy and calm the immune system.

A research group in Erlangen, Germany, has studied this closely. In a 2018 study in Nature Communications, giving mice short-chain fatty acids or a high-fiber diet slowed the cells that break down bone and protected the mice against bone loss, including bone loss driven by inflammation. That is a mouse result, but it gave a clear reason to test fiber in people.

What happened when people with RA ate more fiber

The same group ran a small study, published in Nutrients in 2019. Thirty-six people with rheumatoid arthritis, all continuing their usual treatment, added a high-fiber bar or cereal every day for 28 days. Afterward, they had more regulatory T cells, the immune cells that keep inflammation in check. Blood markers of bone breakdown fell, and patients reported better physical function and quality of life.

A follow-up study in 2020 gave 29 patients daily high-fiber bars for 30 days. Blood levels of short-chain fatty acids rose and several inflammatory signals linked to arthritis fell.

These results are encouraging, but both studies were small, short and had no comparison group. The authors themselves called for properly controlled trials. For now, more fiber is a safe, sensible step with early evidence behind it, not a proven treatment.

Probiotics: a small effect, mixed results

Several randomized trials have given people with rheumatoid arthritis probiotic capsules, usually Lactobacillus strains. A 2022 meta-analysis in Nutrients pooled eight of these trials, covering 344 patients.

Probiotics lowered C-reactive protein, a blood marker of inflammation, by about 3 mg/L on average. But when the analysis was limited to the better-quality trials, there was no clear improvement in overall disease activity, measured by the standard DAS28 score. The trials used different strains, doses and lengths, which makes them hard to compare. The authors concluded that the evidence is not yet strong enough to recommend probiotics to control rheumatoid arthritis.

That doesn't mean probiotics are useless or unsafe for most people. It means no specific product has been shown to reliably reduce joint symptoms.

The Mediterranean-style diet

Whole-diet changes have a slightly longer track record. In a Swedish trial published in 2003 in the Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases, 51 people with rheumatoid arthritis were randomly assigned to a Cretan Mediterranean diet or their usual Western diet for 12 weeks. The Mediterranean group reported less disease activity, better physical function and more vitality. The control group didn't change. The trial was small and not blinded, so the results are best seen as promising.

A more recent Swedish trial called ADIRA, published in 2020 in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, had 50 patients eat an anti-inflammatory diet for 10 weeks and a typical Swedish diet for another 10 weeks, in random order. The anti-inflammatory diet was rich in fish, legumes, whole grains, fruit and vegetables, and included probiotics. Disease activity fell during the anti-inflammatory period but not the control period. However, in the main analysis the difference between the two diets was not statistically significant.

Taken together, eating Mediterranean-style may help some people feel a bit better. It also lowers heart disease risk, which matters because rheumatoid arthritis raises that risk.

Fermented foods

There are no good trials of fermented foods specifically in rheumatoid arthritis. The best evidence comes from healthy adults. In a 2021 Stanford study in Cell, 36 healthy people were randomly assigned to eat more fermented foods, such as yogurt, kefir, kimchi and kombucha, or more fiber for 10 weeks. The fermented-food group gained gut microbial diversity, and 19 inflammatory proteins in their blood went down, including interleukin-6, a key driver of rheumatoid arthritis. The high-fiber group didn't see those changes over that time.

That is a reason to think fermented foods might help, not proof that they do in rheumatoid arthritis.

Keep your treatment. Disease-modifying drugs prevent permanent joint damage, and no diet or supplement has been shown to do that on its own. Talk to your rheumatologist before changing anything about your medication, and treat diet changes as something you add, not something you swap in.

A practical approach

Based on the evidence so far, a reasonable plan looks like this: eat more fiber from vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds; lean toward a Mediterranean pattern with fish and olive oil; add fermented foods you enjoy; and look after your gums, since gum disease is linked to rheumatoid arthritis. If you want to try a probiotic, tell your doctor, especially if you take drugs that suppress the immune system. Track how you feel, and give any change a few months.
