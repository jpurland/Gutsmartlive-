Gut bacteria, blood pressure and cholesterol: what helps

7 minute read

Part 1 looked at TMAO, a compound gut bacteria make that is linked to heart disease. This part turns to the other side: the ways a well-fed gut may help blood pressure and cholesterol, how big those effects really are, and what is worth doing.

Fiber, acetate and blood pressure in mice

When gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids such as acetate, propionate and butyrate. These compounds act as signals throughout the body, including in blood vessels and the kidneys.

A 2017 study in Circulation from Francine Marques and colleagues in Melbourne tested this in mice with a form of high blood pressure. Mice on a high-fiber diet, or given acetate directly, had lower blood pressure than mice on a standard diet. They also had less scarring and thickening of the heart muscle, two early steps toward heart failure. Fiber shifted their gut bacteria toward acetate-producing species.

That was in mice. The real question is whether the same thing happens in people.

The first human test

The same group ran a small trial, published in 2023 in Nature Cardiovascular Research. Twenty adults with untreated high blood pressure ate food containing 40 grams a day of a special resistant starch built to release acetate and butyrate in the gut, or a placebo, for three weeks each. On the fiber, their 24-hour systolic blood pressure, the top number, fell by about 6 mm Hg, and blood levels of acetate and butyrate rose.

That's a meaningful drop, but this was a short, small trial using a specially designed fiber, not ordinary food. Larger, longer studies are needed before anyone can say how well it works.

The bigger picture on everyday fiber

The broadest human evidence comes from a 2019 review in The Lancet, commissioned by the World Health Organization. It pooled 185 long-term observational studies and 58 clinical trials. People who ate the most fiber had a 15 to 30 percent lower risk of death and of heart disease, stroke and type 2 diabetes than those who ate the least. The best results came at roughly 25 to 29 grams a day or more. Most adults eat less than 20.

In the clinical trials, more fiber modestly lowered total cholesterol and systolic blood pressure. The observational findings show association, not proof, but they line up with the trials and the animal work.

Fermented dairy and probiotics

Here the effects are real but small.

Blood pressure. A 2014 meta-analysis in Hypertension combined 9 trials with 543 people, most using fermented milk or yogurt. Probiotics lowered systolic pressure by about 3.6 mm Hg and diastolic by about 2.4 mm Hg on average. Benefits showed up mainly when people took them for at least 8 weeks, at higher doses, and with several bacterial species rather than one.

Cholesterol. A 2015 meta-analysis of 30 trials with about 1,600 people found probiotics lowered LDL ("bad") cholesterol by about 7 mg/dL on average. Results varied widely between studies, and the authors concluded the evidence was not yet strong enough to recommend probiotics for cholesterol. That's far smaller than what cholesterol medication typically achieves.

Don't swap your medication for yogurt. These effects are modest and can add to good care, not replace it. Never stop or change blood pressure or cholesterol medication on your own. Talk to your doctor before making changes, especially if you take several medicines.

Calming inflammation

Inflammation plays a part in artery disease. A 2021 Stanford study in Cell randomly assigned 36 healthy adults to eat more fermented foods or more fiber for 10 weeks. The fermented-food group worked up to about six servings a day of foods like yogurt, kefir, kimchi and kombucha. Their gut bacteria became more diverse, and 19 inflammatory proteins in the blood went down, including interleukin-6. The high-fiber group did not see those drops over the 10 weeks.

The study didn't measure heart outcomes, and it was small. But it is one of the clearest human signals that diet can lower inflammation through the gut.

A practical plan

  • Raise fiber gradually toward 25 to 30 grams a day from beans, lentils, oats, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds. Add a few grams a week so your gut can adjust.
  • Add fermented foods daily. Plain yogurt or kefir with live cultures, sauerkraut, kimchi or miso. Choose low-sugar versions and watch the salt in pickled foods if you have high blood pressure.
  • Eat less red and processed meat. It lowers TMAO and fits long-standing heart advice.
  • Keep the basics. Less salt, regular movement, good sleep, not smoking. These have far more evidence behind them than any single gut fix.
  • Track your numbers with your doctor. Check blood pressure and cholesterol, and let your doctor decide whether any medication should change.

The honest summary: the gut is one part of heart health, not a shortcut around it. A fiber-rich, fermented-food-friendly diet gives your gut bacteria what they need, and its effects add up on top of good medical care.
