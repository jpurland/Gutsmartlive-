Fatty Liver Disease Part 2 of 2

Protecting your liver through your gut

6 minute read

Part 1 showed that the liver sits directly downstream of the gut, and that gut bacteria can push the liver toward storing fat. The encouraging part is that fatty liver is often reversible, especially in its early stages. Here is what the evidence supports, starting with the steps that have the strongest proof.

1. Losing weight is still the most powerful tool

No supplement comes close to the effect of weight loss. In a 2015 study in Gastroenterology, 293 people with the inflamed form of fatty liver (now called MASH) followed a diet and exercise program for a year and had liver biopsies before and after. Among those who lost 10 percent or more of their body weight, 90 percent saw the inflammation resolve and 45 percent saw liver scarring improve. Among those who lost less than 5 percent, only 10 percent saw the inflammation resolve.

Most people in the study didn't reach the 10 percent mark, which is a reminder that it's hard. But even smaller losses helped, and the more weight people lost, the more their livers improved.

2. Cut back on added sugar, especially in drinks

Fructose, half of table sugar and the main sugar in high-fructose corn syrup, is especially easy for the liver to turn into fat. The gut is involved here too. Mouse studies from Princeton found that the small intestine handles modest amounts of fructose, but a large dose, like a sugary drink on an empty stomach, overflows to the liver and the colon. A 2020 follow-up in Nature found that in mice, gut bacteria convert fructose into acetate, which the liver then uses as a building block for fat. Wiping out the bacteria sharply reduced that fat production after a large fructose dose.

In people, the direct test came in a 2019 trial in JAMA. Forty teenage boys with fatty liver were randomly assigned to a diet very low in added sugar or their usual diet. After eight weeks, liver fat in the low-sugar group fell from 25 percent to 17 percent, while the comparison group barely changed. It was a small, short trial in adolescents, but the result was clear.

Practical steps: drop sugary sodas, sweet teas and juice first, then cut back on sweets and packaged foods with added sugars. Whole fruit is fine; its fiber slows how fast the sugar arrives.

3. Feed your gut fiber and fermented foods

Fiber from vegetables, beans, whole grains, nuts and seeds feeds the bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, which help keep the gut lining tight. A leaky gut lining is one of the ways bacterial products reach the liver. Ultra-processed foods tend to be low in fiber and high in sugar and refined starch, so swapping them for whole foods helps on both fronts.

Fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi and kombucha are another option. In a 2021 Stanford study in Cell, healthy adults who added several servings of fermented foods a day for ten weeks had more diverse gut bacteria and lower levels of 19 inflammatory proteins in their blood. That study wasn't about the liver, and no trial has yet shown fermented foods reverse fatty liver. But they are a low-risk way to support a healthier gut. Watch for added sugar in flavored yogurts and kombucha.

4. Probiotics: modest, mixed results

A 2019 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition combined 21 randomized trials with 1,252 people taking probiotics or synbiotics (probiotics combined with the fiber that feeds them). On average, these lowered the liver enzyme ALT, a marker of liver irritation, and improved liver stiffness and fat on ultrasound. But the trials varied widely, many were small or had design weaknesses, and few used liver biopsies.

One of the best-designed trials, published in Gastroenterology in 2020, gave 104 adults a synbiotic or a placebo for about a year. The synbiotic changed their gut bacteria, but it did not reduce liver fat or scarring more than placebo. Liver fat fell in both groups.

The honest summary: probiotics may help a little, but the evidence isn't strong enough to rely on them, and they are no substitute for diet changes and weight loss.

Nothing here is a cure. Diet and gut-focused changes can reduce liver fat and, with enough weight loss, even improve scarring. But fatty liver disease needs medical follow-up, especially to check for fibrosis.

5. Be careful with alcohol

Alcohol is processed by the liver and also makes the gut lining leakier. With a liver already under strain, the two effects add up. The 2023 guidelines that renamed the disease created a separate category, MetALD, for people with fatty liver who also drink moderate-to-heavy amounts, because the combination is riskier than either alone. If you have fatty liver, ask your doctor how much, if any, is safe for you.

6. Work with your doctor

Fatty liver usually has no symptoms, so it is often found by chance on a blood test or scan. Your doctor can check for scarring with a simple score, a stiffness scan or other tests, and look after the conditions that travel with fatty liver: diabetes, high blood pressure and high cholesterol. Medications are now available for some people with more advanced disease. Don't stop or change any medication on your own. Use the gut-friendly habits above alongside your care, not in place of it.
