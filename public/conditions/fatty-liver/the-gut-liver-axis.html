The gut–liver axis: how gut bacteria feed fatty liver disease

6 minute read

Fatty liver disease is now one of the most common health conditions in the world. A large 2023 review that pooled data on more than nine million people estimated that about 30 percent of adults have it. Most of them don't know, because early fatty liver usually causes no symptoms.

Diet, body weight, blood sugar and genes all matter. But over the past fifteen years researchers have found another player: the bacteria living in your gut. This article explains why, and how strong the evidence really is.

A new name: MASLD

You may know this condition as NAFLD, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. In 2023, liver societies from around the world agreed on a new name after a consensus process involving 236 experts from 56 countries. It is now called MASLD, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease. "Steatotic" simply means fatty.

The new name describes what the disease is, not what it isn't. To be diagnosed with MASLD, a person has fat in the liver plus at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, such as excess waist size, high blood sugar, high blood pressure or abnormal blood fats. The more serious, inflamed form, once called NASH, is now MASH. People with MASLD who also drink a moderate-to-heavy amount of alcohol fall into a separate group called MetALD.

The liver sits downstream of the gut

The reason gut bacteria matter so much to the liver comes down to plumbing. Blood leaving the stomach and intestines doesn't go straight back to the heart. It collects in the portal vein and travels first to the liver. That blood carries nutrients from your meal, but it also carries whatever gut bacteria produce or release.

Normally the gut lining keeps most bacterial material inside the intestine, and the liver filters the small amounts that slip through. When the lining becomes leaky, or the bacterial community shifts, the liver gets a steady dose of inflammatory signals and extra raw material for making fat. Scientists call this two-way connection the gut–liver axis.

Evidence from people: a leakier gut

A 2009 study in Hepatology compared 35 people with biopsy-confirmed fatty liver disease to healthy volunteers. Those with fatty liver had a leakier intestine and more often had bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine. Both were linked to how much fat was in the liver. They were not linked to inflammation or scarring, and the authors were clear that the study couldn't show which came first.

Evidence from mice: bacteria can decide who gets fatty liver

The clearest proof of cause and effect comes from animals. In a 2013 study in the journal Gut, French researchers fed mice a high-fat diet. Two mice gained similar weight, but only one developed high blood sugar and inflammation. The researchers moved gut bacteria from each of these two mice into germ-free mice, which have no bacteria of their own, and fed them the same diet.

Both groups became equally obese. But only the mice that received bacteria from the "unhealthy" donor developed fatty liver. In those mice, the genes that build new fat in the liver were more active. The conclusion: in mice, gut bacteria can help determine whether fatty liver develops, separately from weight gain.

Mice are not people. Transfer experiments in mice show that gut bacteria can cause fatty liver. They don't show how much bacteria contribute in any given person. Human studies so far mostly show associations.

Bacteria that brew alcohol

One of the most striking findings came in 2019 in Cell Metabolism. Researchers in China found strains of Klebsiella pneumoniae that ferment sugar into large amounts of alcohol inside the gut. These high-alcohol-producing strains turned up in up to 60 percent of the people with fatty liver they studied.

When they gave these bacteria to mice, the mice developed fatty liver. Transplanting stool from a patient carrying the strain also caused fatty liver in mice, and removing that one strain before the transplant prevented it. For at least some people, the "non-alcoholic" in the old name may not have been entirely accurate: their own gut bacteria may be supplying the alcohol. This is one population, though, and it hasn't been shown to explain most cases.

A microbial fingerprint of liver scarring

The most dangerous part of fatty liver disease is fibrosis, the scarring that can progress to cirrhosis. A 2017 study in Cell Metabolism sequenced stool samples from 86 people with biopsy-proven fatty liver disease. A pattern of 37 bacterial species separated people with advanced scarring from those with milder disease with high accuracy. People with advanced fibrosis had more Proteobacteria, a group that includes E. coli, and fewer of several beneficial species.

It was a small pilot study, and everyone with advanced scarring was over 60, so age may have played a role. It shows a link, not a cause.

Putting it together

Animal studies show gut bacteria can drive fat build-up in the liver, through leaky-gut inflammation, extra fat-building material and even home-brewed alcohol. Human studies consistently find that people with fatty liver have different gut bacteria and leakier guts, but they can't yet say how much those differences cause the disease. The gut is one part of the story alongside diet, weight, insulin resistance and genes. The useful news is that it is a part you can influence, which is the subject of Part 2.
