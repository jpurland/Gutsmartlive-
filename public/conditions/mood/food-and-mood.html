Mood & the Gut-Brain Axis Part 2 of 2

Food, fermented foods and probiotics for mood: what the evidence supports

6 minute read

If gut bacteria are part of the gut–brain conversation, can changing what you eat change how you feel? It's a fair question, and there are now real clinical trials to look at. The honest answer: food appears to help, probiotics may help a little, and none of it replaces proper care for depression or anxiety.

Diet: the strongest human evidence

The best-known trial is SMILES, published in BMC Medicine in 2017 by Felice Jacka and colleagues in Australia. It enrolled 67 adults with moderate to severe depression, and the diet was added alongside whatever treatment they were already getting. Half met with a dietitian seven times over 12 weeks to shift toward a Mediterranean-style diet: vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, fish, olive oil and nuts, with less sweets, processed meat and fried food. The other half got the same number of social-support visits instead.

After 12 weeks, about 32 percent of the diet group were in remission, compared with 8 percent of the social-support group. That's a meaningful difference. It was also a small study, and people knew which group they were in, so larger trials are still needed.

A 2019 analysis in Psychosomatic Medicine pooled 16 randomized trials with more than 45,000 participants, most of whom did not have diagnosed depression. Improving diet reduced symptoms of depression on average. It showed no clear effect on anxiety.

Diet does many things at once, so these trials don't prove the gut is the reason it helps. But the foods that helped are the same ones that feed a diverse gut: plenty of fiber from plants, beans and whole grains, and fewer ultra-processed foods.

Fermented foods: early, intriguing signals

Fermented foods like yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut and kimchi deliver live microbes along with the food. One early study looked at whether that touches the brain at all.

In 2013, a UCLA team published a small study in Gastroenterology. Healthy women ate a fermented milk product containing several live bacterial strains twice a day for four weeks, ate a non-fermented milk product, or had nothing. Brain scans showed that the fermented-milk group had a calmer response in brain areas involved in processing emotion when shown pictures of angry or frightened faces.

That was a proof of concept. Only 36 women took part, none were depressed, and their moods didn't measurably change. It shows that what's in the gut can affect brain activity in people. It doesn't show fermented foods treat depression.

Probiotics: small and mixed effects

Probiotic supplements aimed at mood are sometimes called "psychobiotics." The results so far are modest.

A 2019 review in Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews pooled 34 controlled trials. Probiotics had small but measurable effects on depression and anxiety overall. The benefit showed up mainly in the few trials of people with clinical symptoms. In healthy people from the general community, which was most of the research, there was no clear benefit. Prebiotics alone (fibers that feed gut bacteria) showed no effect in those short trials.

A 2023 pilot trial in JAMA Psychiatry from King's College London tested a multi-strain probiotic in 49 adults with depression who hadn't fully improved on antidepressants. The probiotic was added on top of their usual medication for eight weeks. The probiotic group improved more than the placebo group on several depression and anxiety scales. The researchers called the results preliminary and said a larger trial is needed.

Different strains do different things, and products vary widely. No single probiotic has been shown to reliably treat depression.

Food supports treatment. It doesn't replace it. Please don't stop or change any medication on your own; work with your doctor on any changes. If you're struggling with depression or having thoughts of harming yourself, reach out to your doctor, or in the US call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), any time.

What's reasonable to try

Given the evidence, these are sensible, low-risk steps that support both your gut and your overall health:

  • Move toward a Mediterranean-style pattern: more vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and fish.
  • Eat more fiber from a variety of plants, which feeds the bacteria that make butyrate.
  • Add fermented foods you enjoy, such as plain yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut or kimchi.
  • Cut back on ultra-processed foods and sugary drinks.
  • If you want to try a probiotic, mention it to your doctor first, especially if you take other medications.

Think of these as one support among several, alongside therapy, medication when it's needed, sleep, movement and people you trust.
