Mood & the Gut-Brain Axis Part 1 of 2

The gut–brain axis: how gut bacteria talk to your brain

6 minute read

Most people have felt "butterflies" before a big moment, or lost their appetite during a hard week. That's the gut and the brain talking. Scientists call this two-way connection the gut–brain axis, and over the past twenty years they've found that the trillions of bacteria living in the gut seem to take part in the conversation.

This is one of the most exciting areas of microbiome research. It's also one of the easiest to overstate. Here is what's known, and how sure we can be.

The lines of communication

The gut and brain are linked in several ways at once.

The vagus nerve. This long nerve runs from the brainstem down to the abdomen. Most of its fibers carry information upward, from the body to the brain, so it works largely as a reporting line about what's happening in the gut.

Chemical messengers. Gut bacteria produce or influence many compounds the nervous system uses, including short-chain fatty acids from fiber and substances involved in serotonin, dopamine and GABA signaling.

The immune system. Most of the body's immune cells sit near the gut. An unhealthy gut can keep the immune system mildly switched on, and that low-grade inflammation has been linked to low mood.

Stress hormones. The traffic runs both ways. Stress changes gut movement and the gut lining, and those changes can shift which bacteria thrive.

The serotonin story, told carefully

You may have read that "most of your serotonin is made in the gut." That's true. More than 90 percent of the body's serotonin is made there, mostly by specialized cells in the gut lining. A 2015 study in Cell from Caltech showed that in mice, certain spore-forming gut bacteria prompt those cells to make serotonin. Mice raised without any gut bacteria made much less, and adding those bacteria back restored it.

There's an important catch. Serotonin made in the gut does not travel into the brain. It mostly acts on the gut itself, on digestion and on blood platelets. So gut serotonin is not the same thing as the "brain serotonin" that antidepressants affect. The gut may still influence mood through the vagus nerve and other routes, but the simple version of the story goes too far.

What animal studies show

Animal experiments give the strongest evidence that gut bacteria can affect behavior.

In a 2011 study in PNAS, mice fed a strain of Lactobacillus rhamnosus showed less anxiety- and depression-like behavior and a smaller stress-hormone response. When researchers cut the vagus nerve, those effects disappeared. That pointed to the vagus nerve as a key pathway, at least in mice.

In 2016, researchers in Cork, Ireland, published a study in the Journal of Psychiatric Research with a striking design. They compared the gut bacteria of 34 people with major depression and 33 healthy people, and found the depressed group had less diverse gut bacteria. Then they transferred gut bacteria from depressed patients into rats whose own microbes had been largely cleared out. Those rats began showing depression-like signs, including losing interest in a sweet reward and more anxious behavior. Rats given bacteria from healthy people did not.

That suggests gut bacteria can play a causal role, in rats. Rats are not people, and these behavior tests are only rough stand-ins for human depression.

What we see in people

The largest human study so far came from Belgium. In 2019, researchers analyzed the gut bacteria of 1,054 people in the Flemish Gut Flora Project and checked their results in a separate Dutch group. Published in Nature Microbiology, it found that two groups of bacteria, Coprococcus and Dialister, were lower in people with depression, even after accounting for antidepressant use. Bacteria that produce butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid made from fiber, were linked to better quality of life.

This is an association, not proof of cause. Depression changes how people eat, sleep and move, and all of those reshape gut bacteria. It's possible the bacteria help drive low mood, that low mood changes the bacteria, or both. Human studies so far can't fully separate these.

Where this leaves us

The gut and brain are clearly connected, and animal studies show gut bacteria can change behavior. In people, the evidence is mostly links and patterns, plus a handful of small trials. It's a promising piece of a bigger picture that also includes genes, life events, sleep, relationships and brain chemistry. The gut is one part of mood, not the whole explanation.

The practical question is whether changing your gut, through food, fermented foods or probiotics, can help how you feel. That's the subject of Part 2.
