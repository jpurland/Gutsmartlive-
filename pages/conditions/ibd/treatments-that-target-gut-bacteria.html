Treatments that target the gut bacteria in IBD

6 minute read

If gut bacteria help drive inflammatory bowel disease, changing them should help. Researchers have tested this in several ways: transplanting stool from healthy donors, giving specific probiotics, and using carefully designed diets. Some results are encouraging. None of these approaches is a cure, and the evidence differs a lot between ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease.

Fecal transplants for ulcerative colitis

Fecal microbiota transplantation, or FMT, means delivering screened stool from a healthy donor into a patient's colon. It is well established for recurrent C. difficile infection. For ulcerative colitis, three randomized trials stand out.

In a 2015 Canadian trial in Gastroenterology, 75 adults with active ulcerative colitis received weekly enemas for six weeks, either donor stool or a placebo. At week 7, 24 percent of the FMT group were in remission, compared with 5 percent on placebo.

A 2017 Australian trial in The Lancet used a much more intensive approach: one colonoscopic infusion followed by enemas five days a week for eight weeks, each batch pooled from several donors. Among 81 patients, 27 percent reached the main goal of steroid-free remission with improvement seen on endoscopy, versus 8 percent on placebo.

A 2019 trial in JAMA compared donor stool with the patient's own stool. After eight weeks, 32 percent of the 38 donor-FMT patients were in steroid-free remission, compared with 9 percent of the 35 who received their own stool. At 12 months, 5 of the 12 people who had responded were still in remission.

The pattern is consistent: FMT helps a meaningful minority of people with mild to moderate ulcerative colitis, at least in the short term. Most patients in these trials did not reach remission, the best donor, dose and schedule are still unknown, and long-term data are limited. Evidence for FMT in Crohn's disease is much thinner. FMT is still treated as experimental for IBD and is done through clinical trials or specialist centers, not at home.

Probiotics: helpful in one specific case

The strongest probiotic evidence in IBD is for pouchitis. Some people with ulcerative colitis have their colon removed and a pouch built from the small intestine. That pouch can become inflamed. In a 2004 trial in Gut, 36 people with recurrent pouchitis who had been brought into remission with antibiotics took either a high-dose, multi-strain probiotic called VSL#3 or a placebo. After a year, 85 percent on the probiotic were still in remission, compared with 6 percent on placebo.

That result is specific to pouchitis and to that formulation. For active Crohn's disease, a 2020 Cochrane review found only two small randomized trials with 46 participants in total and concluded that the evidence on whether probiotics help is very uncertain.

Diet as treatment in Crohn's disease

Some of the most interesting results in Crohn's come from diet, especially in children. Exclusive enteral nutrition means drinking a complete liquid formula and eating no regular food, usually for six to eight weeks. It is widely used to bring children with Crohn's into remission, and it changes the gut microbiome substantially, although exactly how it works is still being studied. The drawback is that many children find it hard to stick to.

The Crohn's Disease Exclusion Diet, or CDED, tries to make this easier. It combines partial formula with a structured whole-food diet that removes certain processed ingredients and other foods thought to harm the gut lining or its bacteria. In a 2019 randomized trial in Gastroenterology, 78 children with mild to moderate Crohn's received either CDED with partial formula or exclusive formula feeding. The CDED was tolerated by 97.5 percent of children versus 73.6 percent on formula alone. Remission at week 6 was similar between groups. By week 12, 75.6 percent of the CDED group were in steroid-free remission, compared with 45.1 percent in the comparison group, which had switched to partial formula plus regular food.

These are supervised medical diets, run with a gastroenterologist and dietitian. They are not the same as simply eating healthier or adding fermented foods.

What about fermented foods?

Fermented foods are a healthy part of many diets, and some people with IBD tolerate and enjoy them. But there is little direct trial evidence that yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut or similar foods treat Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis. During a flare, some fermented and high-fiber foods may also worsen symptoms or be unsafe for people with narrowed bowel segments. Ask your care team what fits your situation.

Important: IBD is a serious disease that can cause lasting damage when it isn't controlled. Don't stop or change any prescribed medication on your own. If you're interested in FMT, a probiotic or a dietary approach, bring it to your gastroenterologist. Many of these options work best alongside standard treatment, not in place of it.
