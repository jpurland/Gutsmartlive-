Rheumatoid Arthritis Part 1 of 2

The gut–joint connection: what the research shows

6 minute read

Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease. The immune system attacks the lining of the joints, causing pain, swelling and, over time, damage to cartilage and bone. Genes matter, but they don't explain everything. Something in the environment has to help tip the immune system over the edge.

One candidate researchers have studied for over a decade is the bacteria in the gut and mouth. The findings are intriguing, and less settled than some headlines suggest.

The disease may start far from the joints

People with rheumatoid arthritis often carry tell-tale antibodies in their blood for years before any joint hurts. That raises a question: where does the immune system first go wrong?

One leading idea, laid out in a 2018 review in Nature Reviews Rheumatology, is called the mucosal origins hypothesis. Mucosal surfaces are the moist linings of the gut, mouth and lungs, where the immune system meets huge numbers of microbes every day. The idea is that ongoing irritation at one of these surfaces may train the immune system to make the antibodies that later turn on the joints. It is a hypothesis, not a proven fact, but it has shaped much of the research below.

A gut bacterium that stood out

In 2013, researchers at New York University published a study in eLife comparing stool samples from people with rheumatoid arthritis and healthy people. One species, Prevotella copri, stood out. It was found in about 75 percent of people with newly diagnosed rheumatoid arthritis, compared with about 21 percent of healthy people and about 12 percent of people who had been living with the disease and were already on treatment.

When the team put P. copri into mice, it quickly crowded out other gut bacteria, and the mice became more sensitive to chemically induced gut inflammation. That showed the bacterium could change the gut environment. It did not show that it causes arthritis in people.

Moving from correlation toward cause, in mice

A 2016 study from Japan, published in Arthritis & Rheumatology, went a step further. In a small group of recently diagnosed, untreated patients (17 patients and 14 healthy people), P. copri was dominant in some patients but in none of the healthy people.

The researchers then transferred stool from patients into germ-free mice bred to be prone to arthritis. Mice that received the P. copri-rich stool developed more severe arthritis than mice given stool from healthy people. They also had more Th17 cells, a type of inflammatory immune cell, in the gut. Colonizing the mice with P. copri alone was enough to trigger arthritis.

That is strong evidence that a gut bacterium can drive arthritis in a genetically prone mouse. Whether the same thing happens in humans is still being worked out.

Mouse vs. human. In people, the evidence so far shows that certain bacteria are more common in rheumatoid arthritis. In mice, scientists have shown those bacteria can help cause arthritis. Bridging that gap is the main open question.

The microbiome shifts, and partly shifts back

A large 2015 study in Nature Medicine analyzed stool, dental plaque and saliva from people in China with rheumatoid arthritis and from healthy people. The microbes in all three sites differed from those of healthy people, and the gut and mouth changes tended to mirror each other. Some species were less common in patients and others more common.

The most interesting finding came after treatment. When patients took standard disease-modifying drugs, their microbiome partly moved back toward a healthy pattern. That fits with the idea that the microbiome and the disease influence each other. It also means some of the differences seen in patients may be effects of the disease rather than causes.

A Swiss study published in 2019 looked at people before the disease begins. Among close relatives of people with rheumatoid arthritis, those already showing early signs of autoimmunity were more likely to carry Prevotella bacteria than relatives without those signs. That supports the idea that gut changes can come before the joint disease, though it doesn't prove they cause it.

The gum disease link

The mouth has its own suspects. A hallmark of rheumatoid arthritis is antibodies against citrullinated proteins, proteins that have been chemically altered in a specific way. In 2010, British researchers showed that Porphyromonas gingivalis, a bacterium behind gum disease, carries its own enzyme that makes exactly this change to human proteins. Among the oral bacteria they tested, it was the only one that did so to any real extent.

A 2016 study from Johns Hopkins pointed to a second gum bacterium, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, which triggers the same kind of protein change in immune cells. More than half of the 196 rheumatoid arthritis patients studied showed evidence of past infection with it.

These are plausible mechanisms, not settled causes. But they are one more reason good dental care matters if you have rheumatoid arthritis.

What this means for you

The human evidence shows that the gut and mouth microbiomes look different in rheumatoid arthritis, sometimes before the disease starts, and that treatment partly changes them. Mouse studies show gut bacteria can drive arthritis in prone animals. What isn't proven yet is that changing your microbiome will prevent or treat the disease in people.

That makes the microbiome a promising target, not a replacement for proven treatment. In Part 2, we look at what diet, fiber and probiotics have actually done in trials.
