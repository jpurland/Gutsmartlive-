IBS and the microbiome: what's different in the gut

6 minute read

Irritable bowel syndrome, or IBS, is one of the most common reasons people see a doctor about their gut. It means recurring belly pain tied to changes in bowel habits: diarrhea, constipation, or both. Bloating often comes along for the ride. Standard tests usually come back normal, which can leave people feeling dismissed.

Normal tests don't mean nothing is happening. Researchers now see IBS as a problem in how the gut and brain communicate, and gut bacteria appear to be part of that picture. Here is what the evidence shows, and where it stops.

A disorder of gut-brain interaction

In 2016 the international Rome Foundation, which sets the diagnostic criteria for IBS, updated its framework. It now describes IBS and similar conditions as disorders of gut-brain interaction. The idea is that several things combine: a gut that is more sensitive to normal stretching and movement, changes in how the gut muscles move, low-grade immune activity in the gut lining, changes in gut bacteria, and the way the brain processes signals coming from the gut.

That's why stress can make symptoms flare, and why gut symptoms can affect mood. It runs both ways. It also means there's rarely one single cause, and rarely one single fix.

Clue one: IBS after a stomach bug

Some of the strongest evidence that gut microbes matter comes from people who develop IBS right after a bout of infectious gastroenteritis, often called food poisoning or a stomach bug.

A 2017 analysis in Gastroenterology pooled 45 studies. About 1 in 10 people who had infectious enteritis went on to develop IBS within a year. Compared with people who hadn't been infected, their risk was roughly four times higher. This is called post-infectious IBS.

This doesn't prove that ongoing bacterial changes cause the symptoms. The infection may also leave behind inflammation or nerve sensitivity. But it shows that a disturbance in the gut can trigger a long-lasting problem in people who were fine before.

Clue two: the bacteria look different

Many studies have compared stool samples from people with IBS to people without it. A 2019 systematic review in Gastroenterology gathered 24 such studies. People with IBS tended to have more of some groups, such as Enterobacteriaceae and Bacteroides, and less of others, including Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium, two groups usually considered beneficial. Overall diversity was either lower or unchanged.

The authors were careful to say these are associations. Studies varied a lot, and many didn't match patients and controls by age or sex. It is still unknown whether these differences cause IBS or result from it, from diet changes, or from symptoms themselves.

A 2017 study, also in Gastroenterology, went a step further. In about 140 people with IBS and 56 without, researchers found a pattern of gut bacteria linked to how severe symptoms were. People with severe IBS tended to have less varied gut bacteria and fewer methane-producing microbes. The pattern held up in a second, smaller group. That's a meaningful link, but it is still not proof of cause.

Clue three: an antibiotic helps some people

If bacteria play a role, changing them might help. The antibiotic rifaximin, which stays mostly inside the gut, was tested in two large trials called TARGET 1 and TARGET 2, published together in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2011.

The trials enrolled 1,260 people with IBS without constipation. They took rifaximin or a placebo for two weeks. In the month after treatment, 40.7 percent of the rifaximin group reported adequate relief of their overall symptoms, compared with 31.7 percent on placebo. Bloating relief followed a similar pattern, about 40 percent versus 30 percent.

That's a real effect, but a modest one. Most people didn't get adequate relief, and nearly a third on placebo did. Rifaximin is now an option doctors use for IBS with diarrhea, and it's a prescription decision to make with your doctor.

What about SIBO? Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, or SIBO, means too many bacteria in the small intestine, which normally holds relatively few. Some researchers think it explains a share of IBS. Others are skeptical, partly because the breath tests used to diagnose it can be unreliable. It's an active debate, and online claims often run well ahead of the evidence. If you suspect it, a gastroenterologist can tell you whether testing makes sense.

Putting it together

Gut bacteria are clearly part of the IBS story. IBS can follow an infection, the gut community in IBS tends to look different, and changing it with an antibiotic helps some people. But bacteria are one piece of a larger system that includes nerves, muscles, the immune system and the brain. That's why the most useful approaches tend to combine several angles, which is what the next article covers.
