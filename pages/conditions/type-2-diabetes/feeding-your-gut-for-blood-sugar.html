Type 2 Diabetes Part 2 of 2

Feeding your gut to improve blood sugar

5 minute read

Part 1 showed that gut bacteria help shape how the body handles sugar. The practical question is what you can do about it. The good news is that gut bacteria respond quickly to food. The honest news is that the research is still young, and diet supports diabetes care rather than replacing it.

Before you start: Never stop or change a diabetes medication on your own. Eating more fiber and fewer refined carbs can lower blood sugar, and if you take insulin or drugs like sulfonylureas, that can raise the risk of lows. Talk with your doctor before making big changes, and check your numbers as you go.

Fiber feeds the bacteria that help

The strongest trial so far was published in Science in 2018. Researchers in China randomly assigned 43 adults with type 2 diabetes to one of two diets for three months. Both groups took the same diabetes medication, acarbose, and ate similar amounts of calories, protein, fat and carbohydrate. One group followed standard dietary advice. The other ate a diet very high in a wide variety of fibers from whole grains, prebiotics and other fiber-rich foods.

The high-fiber group saw a bigger drop in HbA1c, the three-month average of blood sugar, and more of them reached an HbA1c below 7 percent. The researchers traced the difference to a specific set of bacteria. Of 141 strains able to produce short-chain fatty acids, the fiber diet boosted 15. People whose diets raised these strains the most improved the most. When stool from the high-fiber group was moved into germ-free mice, those mice had lower blood sugar than mice receiving stool from before the diet.

One study of 43 people is not the last word. But it fits a large body of research linking higher fiber intake with better blood sugar, and it gives a plausible reason why: fiber feeds the bacteria that make short-chain fatty acids, and those compounds help trigger hormones like GLP-1 that steady blood sugar.

Variety matters, not just quantity

Different bacteria prefer different fibers. The Science trial used many types at once, and the authors argued that the mix mattered. In practice that means rotating your sources: oats, barley, beans, lentils, vegetables, nuts, seeds and fruit, instead of relying on one fiber supplement.

Fermented foods: promising, not proven for diabetes

A 2021 Stanford trial in Cell put 36 healthy adults on either a high-fiber diet or a diet rich in fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut and kombucha for 10 weeks. The fermented-food group's gut diversity rose and 19 inflammatory proteins in their blood fell. Low-grade inflammation is part of what drives insulin resistance, so that is encouraging. But the people were healthy, and the study didn't measure diabetes outcomes.

For yogurt specifically, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration allowed a qualified health claim in 2024: eating at least 2 cups (3 servings) of yogurt a week may reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes. The FDA itself describes the supporting evidence as limited. Choose plain, unsweetened versions; flavored yogurts can carry a lot of added sugar.

Artificial sweeteners aren't a free pass

Swapping sugar for zero-calorie sweeteners seems like an obvious move. A 2022 study in Cell suggests it is more complicated. Researchers gave 120 healthy adults who normally avoided sweeteners small daily doses of saccharin, sucralose, aspartame or stevia, below official safety limits, for two weeks. All four sweeteners changed gut bacteria. Saccharin and sucralose also worsened blood sugar responses to a glucose drink.

The effect differed a lot from person to person. When bacteria from the strongest responders were moved into germ-free mice, the mice developed similar blood sugar changes, which points to the gut as the cause. The study was short and in healthy people, so the long-term meaning is unknown. A sensible takeaway is that water, unsweetened tea and coffee are safer bets than diet drinks.

A practical plan

  • Build fiber up slowly. Add one high-fiber food a day each week. Going fast causes gas and bloating, and many people quit.
  • Aim for variety. Try to eat many different plant foods each week: beans, lentils, whole grains, vegetables, nuts, seeds and fruit.
  • Add a fermented food to meals. Plain yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut or kimchi. Check labels for added sugar.
  • Cut back on refined carbs and sugary drinks. They raise blood sugar directly and feed your gut little.
  • Go easy on artificial sweeteners, especially saccharin and sucralose.
  • Watch your own numbers. Because responses vary, a glucose meter or monitor, used with your care team, shows what works for you.
  • Keep your doctor in the loop. If your readings improve, your doctor may adjust your medication. That is their call, not a reason to stop on your own.

The bottom line

No food, probiotic or diet cures type 2 diabetes. What the evidence supports is that feeding your gut more fiber and real fermented foods, and less sugar and artificial sweetener, can help blood sugar alongside medical care. It is low-risk, it helps the rest of your health too, and it is something you can start at your next meal.
