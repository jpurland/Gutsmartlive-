Type 2 Diabetes Part 1 of 2

How gut bacteria shape blood sugar and type 2 diabetes

5 minute read

Type 2 diabetes is usually explained through insulin, weight, genes and diet. Those all matter. Over the past fifteen years, researchers have added another piece: the trillions of bacteria living in the gut. The evidence doesn't say bacteria cause diabetes on their own. It does say they are part of how the body handles sugar, and that they respond to what you eat.

People with type 2 diabetes carry a different gut community

In 2012, a team in China published one of the first large DNA studies of gut bacteria in type 2 diabetes in the journal Nature. They sequenced stool samples from 345 people with and without the disease. People with diabetes had fewer bacteria that make butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid that feeds the gut lining and helps calm inflammation. They also had more bacteria that can cause trouble when given the chance.

That was an association, not proof of cause. Diabetes could change the gut, rather than the other way around. And there was a bigger complication, which turned out to be the most common diabetes drug.

Metformin works partly through the gut

Metformin has been the first-line diabetes medicine for decades. A 2015 Nature study that pooled gut data from Danish, Swedish and Chinese groups found that many of the "diabetes" patterns in earlier research were really metformin patterns. Once the drug was accounted for, a smaller signal remained that did look tied to the disease: fewer bacteria that turn fiber into short-chain fatty acids.

A 2017 trial in Nature Medicine went further. Researchers randomly assigned 40 people newly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes to metformin or a placebo for four months. Metformin clearly changed their gut bacteria. When stool from people taking metformin was transferred into germ-free mice, the mice handled glucose better than mice given stool from before treatment.

So part of metformin's benefit appears to travel through the microbiome. Its gut effects may also explain its best-known side effects, such as bloating and loose stools. This is the clearest human-plus-mouse evidence that changing the gut can change blood sugar.

Important: None of this means you should change or stop a diabetes medication. Metformin and other drugs work. Any change to treatment should happen with your doctor.

Why the same meal spikes one person and not another

In a 2015 study in Cell, Israeli scientists tracked blood sugar with continuous glucose monitors in 800 people across nearly 47,000 meals. People's responses to identical foods varied enormously. One person's blood sugar shot up after bread; another's barely moved. A computer model that included each person's gut bacteria predicted these responses better than counting carbohydrates or calories alone, and it held up in a separate group of 100 people.

In a small follow-up trial of 26 people, diets tailored by the model lowered after-meal blood sugar. The participants did not have diagnosed diabetes, and the trial was short. Still, it showed the gut is one reason generic food rules work better for some people than others.

Transplanting bacteria: a real effect, but short-lived

The most direct test of cause is to move bacteria from one person to another. In a 2012 Dutch study in Gastroenterology, men with metabolic syndrome, a common forerunner of type 2 diabetes, received a stool transplant from either a lean donor or from themselves. Six weeks later, the lean-donor group's insulin sensitivity had improved, along with a rise in butyrate-producing bacteria.

A larger 2017 follow-up of 38 men in Cell Metabolism confirmed the six-week improvement. By 18 weeks, though, the benefit was gone and the recipients' gut bacteria had drifted back to where they started. Men who began with less diverse gut bacteria responded best. Stool transplants are not a diabetes treatment. What these studies show is that gut bacteria can shift insulin sensitivity in people, and that lasting change probably depends on daily habits rather than a single intervention.

Putting it together

The human evidence points in one direction. People with type 2 diabetes tend to have fewer fiber-fermenting, butyrate-producing bacteria. A major diabetes drug works partly through the gut. Personal gut makeup helps explain why blood sugar responses to food vary so much. And swapping gut bacteria can nudge insulin sensitivity, at least for a while.

What the evidence does not show is that fixing the gut cures diabetes, or that any supplement replaces medical care. The gut is one lever among several. Part 2 looks at the lever you control every day: what you feed those bacteria.
