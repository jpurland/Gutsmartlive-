TMAO and the gut's role in heart disease

7 minute read

Heart disease is usually explained through cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking and blood sugar. Those remain the big drivers. Over the past 15 years, though, researchers have found that gut bacteria also take part, mainly by turning certain foods into a compound called TMAO.

From dinner plate to bloodstream

Choline, found in eggs, meat and dairy, and L-carnitine, found mostly in red meat, are normal nutrients. Some gut bacteria break them down into a gas-like compound called trimethylamine, or TMA. TMA travels to the liver, where an enzyme turns it into trimethylamine N-oxide, or TMAO.

The key point is that your body can't make much TMAO on its own. When people or mice are given antibiotics that knock down their gut bacteria, TMAO production nearly stops, then returns once the bacteria grow back.

The discovery

In 2011, a Cleveland Clinic team led by Stanley Hazen published a study in Nature. They screened the blood of heart patients and found that TMAO stood out. In a group of 1,876 people having heart checkups, higher TMAO went along with more coronary and peripheral artery disease, even after accounting for usual risk factors.

They then moved to mice bred to develop clogged arteries. Feeding these mice extra choline or TMAO increased plaque in their arteries. When antibiotics wiped out the gut bacteria, extra choline no longer increased plaque.

Following patients over time

A 2013 study in the New England Journal of Medicine followed 4,007 people who had a planned heart catheterization. Over three years, those with the highest TMAO levels had about 2.5 times the risk of a heart attack, stroke or death compared with those with the lowest levels. In the same paper, healthy volunteers who ate two hard-boiled eggs showed a rise in TMAO afterward, and that rise was largely blocked while they took antibiotics.

Later that year, a study in Nature Medicine looked at L-carnitine. Meat eaters made much more TMAO after a carnitine dose than long-term vegans and vegetarians, whose gut bacteria appeared to have lost much of the ability. In mice, carnitine-rich feed roughly doubled artery plaque, and antibiotics prevented it.

A 2017 analysis that pooled 19 studies with more than 19,000 people found that high TMAO was linked to about a 62 percent higher risk of major heart events and a 63 percent higher risk of death from any cause.

How TMAO might do harm

Lab and animal work points to a few routes. In mice, TMAO helped immune cells in artery walls fill with cholesterol and become "foam cells," the building blocks of plaque. A 2016 study in Cell found that TMAO made platelets, the cells that form blood clots, more reactive, both in lab tests and in mice. In people, higher TMAO tracked with greater clotting potential.

There is also a broader inflammation link. Artery disease is now understood partly as an inflammatory disease. In the 2017 CANTOS trial of 10,061 heart-attack survivors, a drug that calmed one inflammatory pathway, without lowering cholesterol, cut repeat heart events by about 15 percent. That trial did not involve the gut. But it shows why researchers take seriously anything, including signals from gut bacteria, that keeps the body's inflammation turned up.

Association is not proof. Most of the human evidence on TMAO shows that it travels with heart disease. Much of the evidence that it causes heart disease comes from mice.

The open debate

TMAO is not settled science, and it's fair to lay out the doubts.

Kidneys clear TMAO. When kidney function drops, TMAO builds up. Kidney disease also raises heart risk on its own, so high TMAO may partly be a sign of weaker kidneys rather than a separate cause.

Genetic studies are mixed. A 2019 study in Diabetes used genetic data, a method called Mendelian randomization, to test the direction of cause. It found no clear link between genetically higher TMAO and heart disease or stroke. It did find that type 2 diabetes and kidney disease appear to raise TMAO, which suggests some of the link may run backwards.

A reasonable reading: TMAO is a real gut-made compound that is consistently linked to heart risk in people and can drive plaque and clotting in mice. Whether lowering it protects human hearts has not been tested in a large trial.

What diet does to TMAO

Diet clearly moves TMAO. In a 2019 trial in the European Heart Journal, 113 healthy adults rotated through a month each of red meat, white meat and non-meat protein. On the red meat diet, TMAO rose about threefold on average, and it fell again once people switched off red meat.

Researchers are also testing ways to block the bacterial enzymes that make TMA. In mice, a compound called DMB, found in small amounts in some olive oils, lowered TMAO and reduced plaque. It has not been shown to help people.

None of this means eggs or meat are poison, or that a TMAO test should guide your care on its own. It adds one more reason to the long-standing advice for heart health: more plants and fiber, less red and processed meat.
