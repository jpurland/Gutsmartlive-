Obesity & Your Gut Part 1 of 6

Obesity is a disease, not a character flaw

7 minute read

For decades the story was simple: people with obesity eat too much and move too little, so they need more willpower. That story is still everywhere, from doctors' offices to diet ads. It's also out of step with what medicine now knows about how the body regulates weight.

Medicine changed its position

In 2013 the American Medical Association voted to recognize obesity as a disease that needs treatment and prevention, not a lifestyle choice. The World Health Organization classifies it as a chronic disease as well.

In 2025 a global commission published in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology went further. Backed by dozens of medical organizations, it separated "clinical obesity," where excess fat is already harming organs and daily function, from "preclinical obesity," where risk is rising but disease hasn't set in. The commission also argued that BMI alone is a poor test and that doctors should look at where fat sits and what it is doing to the body. That fits what this site focuses on: visceral fat around the organs matters far more than the number on the scale.

Weight is strongly inherited

Twin studies consistently estimate that genes explain roughly 40 to 70 percent of the differences in body weight between people. A landmark 1986 adoption study in the New England Journal of Medicine found that adults adopted as babies ended up with body weights that tracked their biological parents, not the parents who raised them and fed them. A later study of identical twins raised in separate homes found their BMIs were nearly as alike as twins raised together.

Genes don't make obesity inevitable. They do mean two people can eat the same diet and end up in very different places.

The body defends its weight

Body fat is not passive storage. It's an organ that sends hormonal signals, like leptin, to the brain, and the brain uses those signals to steer hunger and energy use. When weight drops, the brain responds by raising hunger and lowering how many calories the body burns. Rare genetic defects in this system cause severe obesity in childhood, which shows how much of weight control runs below conscious choice.

That's why "just eat less" so often fails over the long run. People aren't failing a test of character. They're fighting a system built to protect stored energy. Part 4 of this series looks at that fight in detail.

Where the gut comes in

One of the biggest shifts in the science is the discovery that the trillions of bacteria in your gut take part in this regulation. They influence how many calories you pull from food, how inflamed your body runs, how your appetite hormones behave and how readily you store fat. In animal studies, simply transferring gut bacteria from an obese donor is enough to cause more fat gain on the same diet.

That makes obesity, in part, a disorder of a body system that has been knocked out of balance. Diet, medications, stress and modern food processing all shape that system, and it can be changed. The rest of this series lays out the evidence.

Blame makes it worse

Weight stigma isn't just unkind. Research reviews find that people who experience weight bias, including from health professionals, are more likely to avoid medical care, eat more under stress and gain weight over time. In 2020 an international group of experts, supported by more than 100 medical and scientific organizations, published a consensus statement calling for an end to weight stigma and to the idea that obesity is purely a matter of personal choice.

The takeaway: obesity isn't a personal failing. It's a chronic condition shaped by genes, hormones, environment and your gut bacteria. You're not to blame for it, and you still have real ways to change it.
